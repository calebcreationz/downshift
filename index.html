Downshift

A tenth of the price, until a rule had two parts

Downshift audits which model can take over an agent’s decisions on SERV Reasoning, then checks the cost against the bill. These are first results from invented expense claims. A cheap model matched a pricier one on one policy and slipped on another, so read them as measurements of two tasks, not a promise.

What it means per month

Each line shows monthly cost and the share of right answers. The summary picks the cheapest model that scored 95% or better. Costs use about 600 input tokens per decision. Yours will differ.

What the audit found

On one policy a tenth of the price matched, on another it did not

On Policy A, Luna and Mini both scored 60 out of 60, for $0.0067 and $0.068. A 17-rule policy on 40 other claims also gave 40 out of 40 for all four models, with Luna at $0.0064 and Mini at $0.058. On Policy B the order changed. Gemini 3.8 Flash scored 40 out of 40 for $0.025, Mini 37 for $0.049, Nano 35 for $0.015 and Luna 34 for $0.0055. Luna’s 34 came up in two separate runs. The best buy depended on the policy, which is why Downshift tests your own rules.

Short policies broke the answer format, and one line fixed it

In the first sweep, models often decided correctly but not in the one word we asked for. At 9 rules, strict parsing gave Nano 4 out of 40 and Luna 20, because replies began “APPROVED”, “DECISION” or JSON. We added one line: reply with only the verdict word, with no other text and no JSON. Bad-format replies fell to zero in all seven runs with that line, across four models. Nano went from 4 to 35 at 9 rules and from 21 to 40 at 5 rules. A retry for bad format was built in and never triggered. A gate should still check the format.

What was left was half a rule

Luna’s six misses on Policy B were real. Five were claims paid by company card with a total over $300. The policy escalates only if the card is personal and the total is over $300. Luna escalated anyway, as if the rule had only its second half. The sixth was a total of exactly $1,000.00 against “over $1,000”. Mini over-escalated three times on the same set and Gemini never did. We checked each of Luna’s six against the rule by hand, and the reference answers were right every time.

The raw switch does not switch

SERV’s docs describe a request header that turns the reasoning layer off, so a model can be compared with and without it. On chat completions it made no difference. We sent the same one-line request to GPT-5.4 Nano four ways: no header, then three spellings of the header. Every request reported 197 input tokens. In the Playground, raw mode used 172 input tokens where SERV used 385 for the same prompt, so the layer is visible when it is off.

Because of this, the results here compare models with each other. They do not show what SERV adds over calling a model directly. We reported the header to the OpenServ team and had no answer on it when this page was written.

The cost estimate was wrong once, and the bill caught it

Downshift estimates cost from reported token counts and listed prices. For Gemini 3.8 Flash we first used another Gemini model’s price, so the estimate was about $0.07 where the console billed $0.44 across 283 requests. For Mini, Nano and Luna, the estimates came in 5 to 30 percent under the billed totals ($0.28, $0.16 and $0.06, which also include playground tests and retries). Gemini’s figures here use its listed $1.00 and $4.50 per million tokens. Downshift treats the bill as the source of truth.

What this does not show

  • It is two invented policies with 40 to 100 claims per run. Luna’s Policy B score repeated exactly, but Mini’s 37 and Gemini’s 40 are single runs, so treat the gap between them as a hint.
  • Answers are computed by code from the policy, so no one labelled anything, but the policies are simpler than real company rules.
  • There is no raw baseline, so nothing here shows what SERV adds over a direct call.
  • The 17-rule set had only 4 APPROVE cases out of 40, so over-escalation would barely show there. Mini was not run on the 5- and 13-rule sets, and Gemini’s cost at 17 rules is not shown.

Run it yourself

The harness is in this repo. It needs a SERV API key and a few tenths of a dollar of credit, and runs in a Colab notebook. harness_60cases.py produced Policy A. harness_sweep.py is the policy-size sweep. harness_format_fix.py runs the format fix and the Policy B comparison. The README has the details.
